Here is the design.

**Shape.** This is not a chat assistant that responds when spoken to — it is a standing‑job agent: something that watches two declared sources (the calendar feed and the inbox, each on its own poll or webhook cadence), holds a catalogue that maps every condition it can recognize to one of exactly four verdicts, and keeps a ledger with one row per open scheduling matter. Nothing it reads is ever treated as an instruction by itself — a calendar invite or an email is data about the world, not a command; only what you told it in advance, or what you say to it directly and verifiably, carries authority.

**Deciding act / ask / hand off / ignore.** Every incoming event — a new invite, a reply, a reschedule request, a cancellation — gets classified against the catalogue, and an event the catalogue doesn't recognize defaults to *ask*, never to a guess.

- *Act* covers only things that are reversible, non‑committing, or already inside a scope you set up ahead of time — not something the agent decides case by case while you're gone. Concretely: acknowledging a request ("looking at calendars, will confirm shortly"), proposing time slots when someone asked for your availability and the slots come from a pre‑cleared availability template with no conflicts, confirming a meeting that already matches a hold you approved, or declining an obvious duplicate of an event already on the calendar. These are drafted and sent without a per‑instance check because the request itself, or your standing configuration, already is the approval.
- *Ask* covers real ambiguity: two things now compete for one slot, a counterpart pushes back a second time on a proposed time, someone asks for a new recurring commitment, a request touches travel or cost, or a meeting you'd flagged as important is being moved. The agent sends you one bounded question — the concrete options, a stated default it will take if you don't answer, and a deadline — and if you don't respond by the deadline it holds; it never resolves silently to its best guess.
- *Hand‑off* covers anything that is effectively your identity or judgment and not a scheduling decision at all: a request for personal, medical, financial, or travel detail; anything wrapped in urgency pressure ("call me right now, it's about the deal"); a sensitive personal or family matter; a request from a new counterpart to commit to a legal or financial term embedded in the thread. The agent stops, names exactly what it found and what step only you can take, and does not attempt a workaround.
- *Ignore* covers what needs no action and no report: newsletters, automated calendar system notifications, an invite for something already declined by a standing rule, spam. It's logged, not answered.

**What it may send while you're unreachable.** Sending is bounded by a grant you set up in advance, not improvised in the moment — a scoped rule keyed on who it's talking to (or a class of counterpart, like "existing clients"), what kind of message (acknowledge, propose times, confirm, single follow‑up chase), and which channel, with an expiry and a logged use every time it fires. Inside that grant, the agent may acknowledge, propose pre‑cleared slots, confirm an already‑agreed time, and send one bounded chase if a thread has gone quiet — nothing that commits you to something new outside what the grant already describes, and never anything involving money or a new kind of obligation. A message to someone the agent has never corresponded with on your behalf is always outside every grant, by definition, so a first contact gets a held draft and a note to you, not a send. If a counterpart needs something *now* and you're unreachable and the situation falls outside every grant, the most it does unprompted is a non‑committing holding reply — it keeps the thread alive without deciding anything for you.

**How it identifies itself.** Every message it originates carries a visible line, in the place that channel normally carries such things (a signature or footer line, not buried), stating that it is acting as your scheduling assistant on your behalf — on by default, and not something it turns off on its own judgment. The one exception is a message whose exact text you reviewed and approved yourself before it went out; at that point the words are yours and you can choose to have it sent as you rather than disclosed as the assistant, because that's your identity to spend, not the agent's default. Short of that, it never writes as if it were you.

**How it reaches you.** One channel is treated as verified — reaching you means a message actually lands with your sender authenticated, not a broadcast to whatever inbox it happens to have access to. It's frugal about it: a capped number of messages per day, outside your normal hours only for something it has judged urgent, one running thread per open scheduling matter rather than a new ping for every event inside it, and every message names the specific matter plus the evidence behind it — the actual conflicting invites, the actual reply it got — not just "check your email." A question it asked and didn't hear back on by its stated deadline stays open and waiting rather than getting resolved on its own; a matter only closes in the ledger when it has real evidence the closure happened — a confirmation from the counterpart, an accepted invite — never on the agent's own say-so that it "should be fine."
