Here is the design.

**The decision rule, per event.** Every event coming off the calendar or the inbox — a new invite, a reply, a proposed time change, an approaching start time, a cancellation — gets classified against a fixed, closed set of four outcomes: act, ask, hand off, or ignore. There is no fifth option and no free-text judgment call at this stage; an event that doesn't match anything on the list defaults to ask, never to act. That default is deliberate — the failure mode of an unfamiliar situation should be a question, not a guess.

*Act* covers the routine, low-stakes, already-authorized cases: a meeting request from someone the assistant has scheduled with before lands with no conflict, inside normal hours — accept it and confirm. A counterpart proposes moving an existing meeting to a slot that's actually free — reschedule it. A meeting is thirty minutes out and the other side hasn't confirmed — send the routine "still on?" nudge. These only fire when the specific action they require also falls inside a standing authorization set up in advance (below); if it doesn't, the event downgrades automatically to ask rather than acting anyway.

*Ask* covers anything that changes the outcome and isn't already pre-authorized: two invitations now claim the same slot, a first-time correspondent (someone the assistant has never dealt with) wants time, an existing meeting would need to be cancelled and the other side might reasonably mind, an invite arrives at an odd hour or for an unusually long block. The question sent back is always the same shape: state exactly what's being decided, lay out the concrete options (not "what should I do" but "keep the 2pm and push the 3pm, or the reverse"), name what the assistant would default to if forced to guess, and give a deadline. Nothing gets executed on that default when the deadline passes — a deadline that passes is a hold, not a yes. The assistant keeps a running list of every open question so nothing gets asked twice and nothing silently expires unanswered.

*Hand off* covers anything that is the owner's identity or the owner's money, not the assistant's to touch under any circumstance: a link or request that looks like a password reset, a one-time code, or an account-recovery step; a thread that turns into "let's discuss the deposit" or any financial commitment; a cancellation of something with real relationship stakes (a one-on-one with a superior, a client commitment) rather than a routine sync. Here the assistant does nothing but stop, name exactly what step is being asked for and who is asking, and wait for the owner to do it personally — no summary substitutes for the owner's own action on these.

*Ignore* covers noise: cold outreach from strangers pitching a "quick 15 minutes," calendar spam, a reply of "sounds good" to a meeting that's already confirmed and changes nothing. These get logged and dropped, with no message sent anywhere.

**What it may send while the owner is unreachable.** Drafting is always free — the assistant can compose a reply, hold it, and queue it for review at any time, unreachable or not. Actually sending, unattended, is limited to a standing rule the owner sets up in advance, not a case-by-case judgment made in the moment: a specific counterpart or class of counterpart (say, "people I've met with before"), a specific kind of scheduling action (confirming, rescheduling, chasing a reply), and a specific channel, with an expiry date on the whole rule. Anything that falls inside that rule goes out and gets logged; anything that falls outside it — a brand-new correspondent, a request involving money, anything touching a credential or an identity step, anything with content more sensitive than "here's a time" — is held for the owner regardless of how long they've been offline. Being unreachable for six hours does not widen what the assistant is allowed to send; it only means the backlog of questions grows until they check in.

**How it identifies itself.** Because it is sending from the owner's own address, the recipient has no way to tell from the sender field alone that they're dealing with an assistant rather than the person. So every message it sends carries a plain, visible line saying so — something to the effect of "sent on [owner]'s behalf while scheduling; they'll follow up personally if anything changes." This is on by default for every message the assistant originates on its own initiative. The one exception is a message the owner has personally reviewed and approved word-for-word before it goes out — at that point it is genuinely the owner's message, the owner chose the exact text, and the disclosure line can be dropped because the owner is the one actually speaking. An assistant quietly impersonating the owner with nobody able to tell the difference is exactly the failure this line exists to prevent.

**How it reaches the owner.** It does not use the inbox it's managing as the channel to reach the owner — if the owner is already not checking that inbox for hours, a message left there is not a channel, it's another item in the same backlog. It uses a separate channel the owner actually checks (a phone, a messaging app — whatever is fastest for them), and it only ever writes to that channel, never to a third party. It keeps one open thread per open question, not a running stream, so the owner comes back to a short, countable list rather than a flood: what's being decided, what the options are, what the default would be, and by when. There's a daily cap on how many times it reaches out, so a bad afternoon of ambiguous invites doesn't turn into constant pinging. It stays inside normal hours unless a question is genuinely time-critical — a same-day double-booking, a meeting starting soon with no response — in which case it's allowed to break quiet hours specifically because the cost of waiting is higher than the cost of the interruption. When the owner replies, that answer resolves the specific thread it was sent for and nothing else; a question the owner never answers stays open and gets nudged again within the same daily budget rather than being resolved on the assistant's own authority. And nothing gets marked done on the assistant's say-so — a meeting is only "confirmed" when the other side has actually confirmed it, not when the assistant has sent something and moved on.
