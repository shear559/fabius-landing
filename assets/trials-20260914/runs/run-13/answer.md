Here is a design for that assistant, organized around the four questions you raised.

**How it classifies each event.** Every incoming calendar change or email that touches scheduling gets scored on three things: how reversible the action is, how much it would commit you to something beyond time and place, and how well the assistant can verify who it's dealing with and what they actually want. Those three scores sort the event into one of four lanes.

It acts on its own only when all three are favorable: the action is trivially undoable (accepting a meeting, sending a reschedule request, confirming a slot you already blessed), it commits you to nothing but a time and place, and the counterpart is a known, low-risk contact — someone on an existing thread, a recurring internal meeting, a sender whose intent is unambiguous ("does 3pm work?" with no other content). Routine acknowledgments and logistics that never leave the boundaries of scheduling fall here.

It asks you first — meaning it drafts the action and holds it rather than sending anything — whenever the action is only semi-reversible, touches something beyond pure logistics, or the intent is plausible but not certain. Declining or canceling a meeting with an external party, resolving a double-booking between two people who both have a legitimate claim on your time, replying to a first-time contact, or anything where the email contains language that sounds like a commitment (price, scope, a promise, personal information) all sit here. The assistant prepares the draft so that when you do resurface, approving it is one action, not a research project.

It hands off — routes to a designated human, with full context, rather than acting or waiting on you — when the matter isn't really a scheduling decision at all: a negotiation, a substantive question embedded in what looked like a scheduling email, anything that reads as an emergency, or a sender you've flagged as needing special handling. Handing off means telling the counterpart honestly that this has been routed to someone else for a response, not inventing an answer and not staying silent.

It ignores the event — takes no action and doesn't surface it — when there's nothing to decide: automated notifications, marketing mail, threads already resolved, calendar entries that are purely informational, or categories you've pre-marked as noise. Ignoring is a deliberate, logged non-action, not a fallback for uncertainty; uncertainty routes to "ask," not "ignore."

**What it may send while you're unreachable.** The allowed set is narrow and defined by content, not by urgency: acknowledgment that a message was received and a response is coming; proposing or confirming times drawn only from availability windows you've already authorized; sending or updating a calendar invite for a time that's already agreed; and polite holding language when a reply is expected but a decision isn't ready. It may never send a cancellation without your stated reason, any commitment beyond time and place, a first reply to someone it can't identify with confidence, or anything that surfaces calendar content unrelated to the matter at hand. If a held "ask" item has a deadline that passes with no response from you, the assistant takes the most reversible fallback available (typically: decline and offer to reschedule, never silently confirm) and reports exactly what it did and why as soon as you're reachable, with a one-step way to undo it.

**How it identifies itself.** It never writes as if it were you typing the words yourself. Every message it sends carries a fixed, honest signature line identifying it as a scheduling assistant acting on your behalf, and on any first contact with a new correspondent that disclosure appears in the body, not just buried in a footer. If anyone asks directly whether they're talking to a person, it says plainly that it's an assistant handling your scheduling and offers to connect them to you or to your designated backup. It doesn't impersonate your voice, apologize on your behalf for personal matters, or pretend authority it doesn't have.

**How it reaches you.** Routine "ask" items are batched into a single periodic digest rather than interrupting you one at a time — you review and approve or reject a short queue when you resurface. A separate, distinct channel is reserved for the small set of things that count as truly urgent (a hard deadline inside a few hours, a hand-off matter that can't wait, anything flagged high-stakes) — this channel needs to be different from the inbox you're already unreachable on, since paging you through the same channel that's silent defeats the point. You can also declare unreachable windows in advance (travel, flights) which tightens the assistant's default behavior automatically — fewer autonomous actions, more hand-offs — for exactly that period, and every action it took while you were gone is logged and summarized for you the moment you're back, whether or not you were paged for it.
